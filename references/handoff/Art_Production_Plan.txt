LANTERN KNIGHT ART PRODUCTION PLAN
Provisional foundation for the fixed camera 2.5D action RPG
6 October 2026

Start with one approved male Lantern Knight, one measurable camera setup and one successful movement and attack test. Only then expand to eight directions and a full animation set. The accompanying JSON files are a proposed art contract and examples of metadata. They do not contain production sprites, measured animation data or an implemented importer.

THE FIRST ART DECISION

The hero is male and carries a sword and lantern. The latest male Knight felt too generic and chunky, so the next design pass should give him a more distinctive identity with lighter armor. Four directions are being explored: lamplighter, exorcist, duelist and occult noble. The existing sheets remain useful style references, but their heavy plate, broad shoulder silhouette, topknot and palette are not locked character requirements.

The old generation briefs and the three substyle comparison specified a female Knight. They are useful visual references, but they are not an approved male model sheet. Male is already the requested direction. The next approval selects a distinctive lighter-armored design and settles his face, build, hair, costume, palette and silhouette, without repeating the male choice.

Recommended finish: Ember Ink's expressive anatomy and selective dark contour, simplified with Silken Shade's flowing cloth. Use Carved Gothic selectively for a few clean hard-surface planes; do not restore the heavy armored silhouette the user wants to move away from. Keep two or three hard shade values per material, clear shapes at gameplay size, matte surfaces and very little ornamental detail. The earlier oxblood, charcoal, bone steel and amber palette is a starting reference only; the lighter character variants may propose a different coherent palette for approval. Avoid grain, paper noise, scratches, tiny buckles and soft airbrushed shading. These inspirations are a visual direction, not permission to copy existing game characters.

Design sheet to approve:
- A front three quarter hero view, true front, back and side design views, all clearly labeled as concept views.
- Sword and lantern close ups, material swatches, hand assignment and any chosen cape or coat attachment.
- A small gameplay size silhouette test. A large attractive illustration alone does not establish readability.
- Anatomical right hand holds the sword; anatomical left hand holds the lantern. Screen left and screen right change as he turns. Do not mirror poses to manufacture missing directions.

WHY A RIG COMES BEFORE HUNDREDS OF FRAMES

For consistent production animation, build a simple 3D character and rig from the approved design, animate it, and render from the locked orthographic camera with a controlled graphic finish. A rig gives repeatable proportions, sword length, hand grips, feet, camera, scale and attachments. Selective contours and broad flat shading can preserve an illustrated appearance. Art direction and cleanup are still required; a rig does not automatically produce the desired style.

Use image generation for the design sheet, costume choices, environment concepts and carefully reviewed isolated static art. It cannot guarantee exact frame registration, anatomy, camera elevation, weapon continuity or smooth motion across hundreds of independently generated images. A generated sprite sheet is a visual proposal until every frame is checked. Do not derive a production animation set by asking for every pose independently.

Hand animated 2D is a valid alternative if a skilled animator owns all views and registration. It trades rig setup for substantially more drawing and consistency work. The proposed first production route is a stylized 3D rig pre rendered to sprites, not a requirement to show realistic 3D materials in the game.

APPROVAL GATES

1. Male design approval
   Review the lamplighter, exorcist, duelist and occult noble variants within this existing design gate. Select one lighter, more distinctive male identity, then approve its silhouette, face, hair, costume, palette, sword and lantern. No mass animation work yet.

2. Camera and scale approval
   Use the same actual 3D proxy, same floor, wall, tree and chest, same world sizes and same orthographic pixel density. Compare camera elevations of 30, 35.264 and 45 degrees above the ground, at 45 degrees azimuth. Include overhead readability, near wall occlusion and mouse aiming space. Do not compare three unrelated AI paintings and call that a camera test. The provisional middle option is the true isometric elevation; the user has approved a fixed camera, not a numerical elevation.
   Approve the target gameplay zoom and visible hero height at the intended window size. A provisional 1.8 unit hero and 1 unit = 1 metre convention make relative scale easy to communicate; they are not measurements taken from the old drawing.

3. One direction motion test
   Render one walk loop and one sword attack in d45, the direction toward the camera, with the approved sword and lantern. Include idle transition, foot contacts, attack anticipation/contact/recovery and the held lantern during the swing. Inspect at native output size and gameplay zoom on dark and light ground. Fix sliding feet, popping outlines, clipping, changing scale and bad grips before expansion.

4. Eight direction motion approval
   Render those approved clips at the eight world yaw directions. Check front, back and both sides; retain equipment handedness. Confirm direction switching and readable back views. A complete eight direction walk does not also supply backward walking and strafing.

5. Remaining actor and small environment kit
   Add idle, run, dodge, hit, death and lantern casting. Then complete one enemy and the few world modules below. Add additional enemies, armor sets and weapons after the first kit works visually.

6. Export and integration review
   Deliver registered RGBA masters, atlas pages, metadata, review movies and the source rig/render scene. Verify against the contract. The user's implementation team owns importer and runtime work; this guide does not start or manage that work.

THE SMALLEST USEFUL KIT

Hero: one male Lantern Knight, sword and lantern baked together into the actor for the first kit. Keep separate rig objects and sockets in the authoring source so future equipment can be produced without redesigning him.
Enemy: one simple skeleton with a distinct narrow silhouette, one short sword and a shield. Begin with idle, walk, attack, hit and death. Its visual proportions, exact size and equipment are a fresh approval, not final assets taken from a multi character concept sheet.
Floor: one square ground material, two quiet variants and a worn edge or small decal. The square texture is mapped onto the XZ floor; it should not contain a perspective diamond painted into the image.
Wall: one straight module, one corner/end treatment, clean top and side planes. Give it an explicit footprint and height. A 3D proxy with artwork on simple surfaces is preferable where the hero can pass behind it.
Tree: one graphic silhouette with a separate foreground canopy if needed for occlusion. Use a stable trunk base anchor and a small ground footprint rather than the entire leaf silhouette as collision.
Chest: closed and open states from the chosen camera, sharing the same root and scale. A small opening sequence can follow if interaction requires it.
Lantern effects: a restrained flame/flicker loop and a short lantern flare. Flare is the proposed first lantern ability. Keep emission separate from opaque actor art and put environmental illumination in a separate pass. A sword trail can be a later secondary effect once contact timing reads without it.

PROVISIONAL ANIMATION BUDGET

All numbers below are starting budgets for review, not a demand for unnecessary frames.
- Idle: 12 drawings per direction, 12 drawings per second, 1.00 second loop.
- Walk: 12 at 15 per second, 0.80 second loop.
- Run: 8 at 16 per second, 0.50 second loop.
- Sword attack: 10 at 20 per second, 0.50 second action.
- Dodge: 8 at 20 per second, 0.40 second action.
- Hit: 4 at 16 per second, 0.25 second reaction.
- Death: 12 at 12 per second, 1.00 second action, then hold the last pose.
- Lantern cast: 12 at 16 per second, 0.75 second action.

That is 78 drawings for one direction, or 624 hero images for eight directions before alternate equipment or extra layers. The first gate needs only the one direction walk and attack test. Sixty frames per second display does not require 60 new sprite drawings per second. World movement and input can remain smooth while animation holds individual drawings. Use time based playback and actual frame durations; do not tie gameplay speed to monitor refresh.

WASD moves and the mouse supplies aim. The smallest proposed art behavior is movement facing during walk/run, aim facing for idle/attack/cast, and a locked aim direction during the committed part of a sword attack. Dodge faces its travel direction. Independent mouse aim remains available while moving; entering an attack or cast uses that aim. The facing policy and short action commitment are proposed implementation defaults, not confirmed user decisions. If the body must always face the mouse while moving in any direction, add backward/strafe locomotion or a deliberately layered rig. Eight facing renders of a forward walk do not solve independent aim and movement by themselves.

REGISTRATION AND ATTACHMENTS

Keep one virtual untrimmed canvas and one projected ground root for the hero across all clips and directions. This root is a rig reference on the floor, not the lowest opaque pixel or whichever foot happens to be planted. The animation can lean, jump, recoil and swing around that root. Do not recenter or rescale each frame to its visible silhouette.

The example uses a 512 by 640 source canvas, ground root at pixel [256, 544], and 256 source pixels per projected world unit. A camera facing card therefore measures 2.0 by 2.5 projected world units. These are an editable calibration fixture, not mandatory production dimensions. Choose the final canvas and source density from the approved projected footprint, motion bounds and gameplay zoom. Keep transparent room for weapons and the cape. If a larger canvas is required, extend it while preserving root and pixel density rather than shrinking the actor.

If an atlas trims transparent pixels, record both the atlas rectangle and its offset inside the original canvas. The frame still uses the original canvas, anchor and scale. A renderer may reconstruct the original canvas or position the trimmed quad with the same offset. It must not silently center the trimmed image.

Keep the sword in the right hand and the lantern in the left through every clip. The right hand may be behind the body from some views. Do not swap hands just to keep both props visible. The first kit's composited actor renders resolve self occlusion automatically. Later modular equipment needs per frame sockets plus correct front/body/back ordering or a shared depth solution; a single generic overlay cannot solve every sword/body crossing.

Export lantern light origin, grip sockets and weapon tip where useful, in explicitly named coordinate spaces. Projected socket pixels are useful for 2D effects. Actor local 3D socket transforms from the rig are needed for consistent world space attachments. Do not invent one from the other. No measured sockets are supplied in the example JSON.

TIMING AND GAMEPLAY BOUNDARIES

Use stable clip names, direction IDs, frame order, frame durations and events. Suggested events include foot_contact_left/right, sword_swing_start, sword_hit_window_open/close, dodge_window_open/close, lantern_flare_release and death_settled. Each event has a unique ID and a time relative to clip start, so skipped display frames do not lose it and a held frame does not repeat it. Loop events include the loop occurrence when deduplicating.

Art events describe animation timing. Their example values are unapproved. Gameplay still decides collision, damage, invulnerability, movement distance and cooldowns. An opaque sword pixel is not a hitbox. Attack validation and one hit per target per swing belong to gameplay. If a clip is interrupted, the runtime must cancel its remaining windows and pending effects rather than leave them active.

Use in place locomotion in the first export, with a measured intended speed to match the feet. Dodge displacement is a separate approved gameplay curve. If root motion is exported later, specify who applies it so movement is not counted twice. Slopes, jumps and elevated platforms are outside this first flat ground art contract.

STATIC ART AND EXPORT QUALITY

Generate or paint clean isolated objects from the approved reference and camera. Keep the floor, walls, objects, character and effects modular. Ask for genuine transparency on cutouts. A dark background or checker pattern painted into the picture is not alpha. Avoid baked floor shadows in the actor/prop color image; use a separate contact shadow asset or shadow proxy. Three.js sprites do not cast geometry shadows automatically.

Keep lossless RGBA PNG masters. Record color space and alpha convention. Use sRGB for color artwork and linear/non color sampling for normal, mask and depth data. Keep emissive effects separate so their brightness is controllable. Validate straight versus premultiplied alpha exactly once in the pipeline; double multiplication causes dark edges. Inspect cutouts over black, white and representative floor colors.

Prefer 2048 atlas pages as a starting point, splitting by actor and useful clip groups. Choose final page size after checking the actual target device limits and memory budget. A 2048 square uncompressed RGBA8 page is 16 MiB before mipmaps, so PNG download size is not runtime GPU memory. Preserve full source canvas metadata while packing trimmed rectangles. Disable rotated packing in the first contract. Add edge extrusion and enough separation for the chosen filtering and mip levels; a tiny fixed gutter is not a universal guarantee against bleed. Verify the packed output at gameplay zoom.

Normal or depth maps are optional later passes from the same rig and exact camera, with a declared coordinate space and value encoding. They are not needed for the first clean style test. A rough AI normal map should not be treated as measured geometry. Dynamic lantern illumination can begin with a controlled ground pool and emission instead of relighting every painted surface.

CAMERA MATH FOR THE ART HANDOFF

Conventions: Y is up; the ground is XZ. Yaw zero points along +Z; positive yaw turns toward +X, with yaw = atan2(directionX, directionZ). Camera azimuth uses the same convention. Camera elevation is measured above the horizontal ground. At azimuth 45 degrees and elevation 35.2643897 degrees, camera offset is proportional to [1, 1, 1].

Direction IDs d00, d45, d90, d135, d180, d225, d270 and d315 mean world yaw. They do not mean screen compass headings. At this camera d45 faces toward the viewer and projects screen down; d225 faces away and projects screen up. A screen input vector must first be mapped to the ground, not mistaken for world XZ.

For camera facing sprite cards, use width = sourceWidth / P and height = sourceHeight / P, where P is source pixels per projected world unit. Local pixel position relative to the root is ((pixelX - rootX) / P, (rootY - pixelY) / P). If using Three.js Sprite.center, its lower left convention means center = (rootX/sourceWidth, 1-rootY/sourceHeight). The example gives [0.5, 0.15].

If implementation instead uses vertical world Y cards, the rendered height is foreshortened by cos(elevation). To reproduce the source image, card height must be sourceHeight / (P * cos(elevation)); width remains sourceWidth / P. The example becomes about 3.06186 units tall at 35.2643897 degrees. Do not apply that correction to an already camera facing card. A vertical proxy measuring 1.8 world units projects to 1.8 * cos(elevation), about 1.46969 projected units. Neither sprite-card height is the physical height of the character.

These formulas handle visual registration, not arbitrary 3D occlusion. Large tree canopies, walls and actors crossing one another need tested ordering and/or explicit occlusion/depth proxies. Approve those cases in the small kit before treating the presentation as production ready.

ACCEPTANCE CHECKLIST

- Distinctive lighter-armored male identity, hand assignment, palette and silhouette approved.
- Camera and zoom approved against the same measured 3D setup.
- Root, canvas, pixel density and camera ID identical across a clip set.
- No per frame auto scale, recentering, clipped sword, detached lantern or changing costume parts.
- Clean loop seam, stable planted feet and readable attack timing at gameplay zoom.
- Every direction is genuinely authored/rendered, not an accidental mirror.
- All frame durations are positive; events fall within their clip; completion and interruption are defined.
- Real alpha, no background residue, no dark/white fringes and no atlas bleed in the tested zoom range.
- Floor seams, wall corners, open chest alignment and near/far tree overlap checked.
- Metadata references only actual delivered files in a production manifest. The example manifest deliberately marks all art as planned or illustrative.
